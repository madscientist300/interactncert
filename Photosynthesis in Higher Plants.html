Topic 1

What we know, and how we found out

Photosynthesis is a physico-chemical process in which green plants use light energy to make organic compounds. It is the basis of all food on Earth and the source of atmospheric oxygen. Two simple experiments show what it needs: a variegated leaf kept in light makes starch only in its green parts, and a leaf half-covered in black paper makes starch only in the exposed half. So photosynthesis needs chlorophyll, light and CO2.

Where it happens: the chloroplast

Mesophyll cells carry many chloroplasts that line up along the walls to catch optimum light. Inside, membranes stack into grana (thylakoids) joined by stroma lamellae. The fluid around them is the stroma.

  • Thylakoid membranes: trap light and make ATP and NADPH (light reaction).
  • Stroma: enzymes that fix CO2 into sugar (dark or biosynthetic reaction).
NEET trapThe "dark reaction" does not need darkness. It runs in the light too, it just does not use light directly. It depends on ATP and NADPH from the light reaction.

Topic 2

Pigments involved in photosynthesis

Paper chromatography separates leaf pigments into four bands. Chlorophyll a is the chief pigment and sits at the reaction centre. The rest are accessory pigments: they absorb wavelengths that chlorophyll a misses, pass the energy on to it, and protect chlorophyll a from photo-oxidation.

Chlorophyll aBright or blue-green. Reaction-centre pigment. About 75% of leaf chlorophyll.
Chlorophyll bYellow-green. Accessory pigment that widens the range of light used.
XanthophyllsYellow. Accessory carotenoid pigment.
CarotenesYellow to yellow-orange. Accessory and protective.

RememberPhotosynthetically active radiation (PAR) is 400–700 nm. The action spectrum and chlorophyll a absorption match closely, which shows chlorophyll a is the main pigment, but they do not match exactly because accessory pigments also contribute.
Topic 3

The light reaction (photochemical phase)

The light reaction includes light absorption, splitting of water, release of oxygen, and formation of the high-energy chemicals ATP and NADPH. Pigments are organised into two discrete light harvesting complexes (LHC) in Photosystem I and Photosystem II, named in the order they were discovered, not the order they work.

Each photosystem has hundreds of pigment molecules forming an antenna, and one chlorophyll a molecule as the reaction centre. In PS I the reaction centre absorbs best at 700 nm (P700); in PS II at 680 nm (P680).

Splitting of water

Water splitting is linked to PS II and happens on the inner side of the thylakoid membrane (lumen side). It supplies the electrons that replace those lost by P680, releases O2 as a by-product, and adds H+ to the lumen.

2H2O → 4H+ + O2 + 4e−

Cyclic vs non-cyclic photophosphorylation

  • Non-cyclic: both photosystems, in series. Makes ATP and NADPH, and releases O2. Happens in grana.
  • Cyclic: PS I only. Electrons return to the chain, so only ATP is made. Happens in stroma lamellae, which lack PS II and NADP reductase. Also when only light beyond 680 nm is available.

Chemiosmotic hypothesis: how ATP is made

Fig 4

ATP synthesis is tied to a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane. Protons pile up inside the lumen for three reasons:

  • Water is split on the lumen side, releasing H+ there.
  • The primary acceptor passes electrons to a hydrogen carrier (PQ), which removes H+ from the stroma and releases it into the lumen.
  • NADP reductase is on the stroma side, so making NADPH removes H+ from the stroma.

Protons flow back to the stroma through CF0, the transmembrane channel. This changes the shape of CF1, which sticks out into the stroma and makes ATP.

Topic 4

The Calvin cycle (biosynthetic phase)

Melvin Calvin used radioactive 14C in algae and found the first CO2 fixation product was a 3-carbon acid, 3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA). Plants that make PGA first are C3 plants. The CO2 acceptor is the 5-carbon ketose sugar ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP), and the enzyme is RuBisCO, the most abundant enzyme on Earth. The cycle runs in all photosynthetic plants, C3 or C4.

Topic 5

The C4 pathway and CAM

Plants adapted to dry tropical regions, such as maize, sorghum and sugarcane, use the C4 or Hatch–Slack pathway. Their first stable product is the 4-carbon oxaloacetic acid (OAA). They tolerate high temperatures, respond to high light intensity, lack photorespiration, and have higher biomass productivity.

The leaves show Kranz anatomy ("kranz" means wreath): large bundle sheath cells form rings around the vascular bundles. These cells have many large chloroplasts, thick walls impervious to gas exchange, and no intercellular spaces.

Two ways to concentrate CO2

In C₄ plants, PEP carboxylase (PEPcase) in mesophyll cells fixes CO₂ and has no oxygenase activity. Mesophyll cells lack RuBisCO. Bundle sheath cells are rich in RuBisCO but lack PEPcase. This division of labour concentrates CO₂ around RuBisCO.

Topic 6

Photorespiration

RuBisCO can bind both CO2 and O2, so it acts as a carboxylase or an oxygenase. It prefers CO2, but the ratio of O2 to CO2 decides which wins. When CO2 is low around the enzyme (hot, dry days with stomata closed), O2 binds instead.

RuBP + O2 → 1 PGA + 1 phosphoglycolate

  • No sugar, ATP or NADPH is made. ATP is actually used, and CO2 is released.
  • Involves three organelles: chloroplast, peroxisome and mitochondrion.
  • It is wasteful, which is why C4 plants, which keep CO2 high around RuBisCO, are more productive in the tropics.

Topic 7

Factors affecting photosynthesis

Internal factors include the number, size, age and orientation of leaves, mesophyll cells and chloroplasts, internal CO2 and the amount of chlorophyll. External factors are light, CO2, temperature and water. Many act together, but at any moment one sets the rate.

Blackman's law of limiting factors (1905)If a chemical process is affected by more than one factor, its rate is decided by the factor nearest its minimal value. Changing that factor alone changes the rate.

Find the limiting factor

Fig 7 · interactive
Limiting factor now

The model is simplified. CO2 in µL/L (ppm). Air today is roughly 400 µL/L (0.04%).

Light

At low intensity, rate rises linearly with light. At higher intensity, other factors limit, so the curve flattens (light saturation). Light saturation occurs at about 10% of full sunlight, so except for plants in shade or dense forest, light is rarely limiting in nature. Very high light can break down chlorophyll.

Temperature

The dark reactions are enzymatic, so they are temperature controlled. Light reactions are temperature sensitive too, but much less so. C4 plants have a higher optimum than C3 plants. Optimum also depends on habitat: tropical plants have higher optima than temperate ones.

Carbon dioxide

CO2 is the major limiting factor. It is only about 0.03–0.04% of air. Raising it to about 0.05% increases fixation; above that it can damage plants over long periods.

  • C3 plants respond to higher CO2; saturation is seen only beyond 450 µL/L.
  • C4 plants saturate at about 360 µL/L.
  • Growers use this in greenhouses: tomatoes and bell peppers are raised in CO2-enriched air for higher yields.

Water

Water stress acts mainly indirectly: it closes stomata, cutting CO2 supply, and wilts leaves, reducing surface area and metabolic activity.
