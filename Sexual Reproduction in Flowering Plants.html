Topic 1

The flower, the stamen and the microsporangium

Flowers are the sites of sexual reproduction in angiosperms. Long before a flower is seen, hormonal and structural changes start the floral primordium. In the flower, the androecium (a whorl of stamens) is the male reproductive organ and the gynoecium is the female one.

A stamen has a long slender filament and a terminal, generally bilobed anther. A typical angiosperm anther is bilobed, and each lobe has two theca: it is dithecous. In transverse section the anther is four-sided (tetragonal), with four microsporangia at the corners, two in each lobe. The microsporangia become pollen sacs, packed with pollen grains.

Microsporogenesis and the pollen grain

The pollen wall

Pollen grains are generally spherical, about 25–50 µm across, with a two-layered wall.

  • Exine: hard outer layer of sporopollenin, one of the most resistant organic materials known. It withstands high temperature, strong acids and alkali; no enzyme that degrades it is known. Pollen fossils are well preserved because of it.
  • Germ pores: apertures in the exine where sporopollenin is absent.
  • Intine: thin, continuous inner layer of cellulose and pectin.

Pollen and people

  • Pollen of many species causes allergies and bronchial afflictions (asthma, bronchitis). Parthenium (carrot grass), which came to India with imported wheat, causes pollen allergy.
  • Pollen is rich in nutrients; pollen tablets and syrups are sold as food supplements.
  • Viability: rice and wheat lose it within 30 minutes; some Rosaceae, Leguminoseae and Solanaceae keep it for months.
  • Pollen can be stored for years in liquid nitrogen (−196 °C) in pollen banks, for crop breeding.
Topic 3

The pistil and the ovule

The gynoecium may be a single pistil (monocarpellary) or many (multicarpellary). Multiple pistils may be fused (syncarpous, e.g. Papaver) or free (apocarpous, e.g. Michelia). Each pistil has a stigma (landing platform for pollen), a style and an ovary. Inside the ovarian cavity (locule) is the placenta, from which the ovules (megasporangia) arise.

Ovules per ovary: one in wheat, paddy, mango; many in papaya, watermelon, orchids.

Topic 4

Megasporogenesis and the embryo sac

Forming megaspores from the megaspore mother cell (MMC) is megasporogenesis. Ovules generally differentiate a single MMC in the micropylar region of the nucellus: a large cell with dense cytoplasm and a prominent nucleus. It divides by meiosis into four megaspores.

In most flowering plants one megaspore is functional and the other three degenerate. Forming the embryo sac from a single megaspore is monosporic development.

Topic 5

Pollination

Male and female gametes are both non-motile. Pollination, the transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma of a pistil, brings them together. Depending on where the pollen comes from, there are three kinds.

NEET trapNot all aquatic plants use water. Water hyacinth and water lily flowers rise above water and are pollinated by insects or wind. In Vallisneria the female flower reaches the surface on a long stalk and male flowers or pollen float to it; in sea-grasses female flowers stay submerged and long, ribbon-like pollen travels inside the water.
RememberAmorphophallus (tallest flower, about 6 feet) offers a safe place to lay eggs. Yucca and its moth cannot complete their life cycles without each other: the moth lays eggs in the ovary locule and pollinates the flower. Visitors that take pollen or nectar without pollinating are pollen/nectar robbers.

Cleistogamy and outbreeding devices

Viola (common pansy), Oxalis and Commelina make two kinds of flowers: chasmogamous (open, with exposed anthers and stigma) and cleistogamous (never open). Cleistogamous flowers are invariably autogamous and give assured seed-set even without pollinators.

Most flowers are bisexual, and continued self-pollination causes inbreeding depression. Plants have evolved devices to discourage it.

Topic 6

Pollen–pistil interaction and artificial hybridisation

Pollination does not guarantee the right pollen. The pistil recognises whether pollen is compatible (right type, same species) or incompatible. Compatible pollen is accepted; incompatible pollen is rejected by preventing germination on the stigma or pollen tube growth in the style. This is a continuous chemical dialogue between pollen and pistil.

After compatible pollination the pollen grain germinates through one of the germ pores. The tube grows through stigma and style to the ovary. If pollen was shed 2-celled, the generative cell divides into two male gametes during tube growth in the stigma; 3-celled pollen carries both gametes from the start. The tube enters the ovule through the micropyle, then one synergid through the filiform apparatus, which guides it. Everything from pollen landing to the tube entering the ovule is pollen–pistil interaction.

RememberPollen germination is easy to see: dust pollen of pea, chickpea, Crotalaria, balsam or Vinca on a drop of about 10% sugar solution; tubes appear in 15–30 minutes.

Topic 8

Post-fertilisation: endosperm, embryo, seed and fruit

Endosperm and embryo development, ovules maturing into seeds, and the ovary into fruit are together the post-fertilisation events.

Endosperm development precedes embryo development. The primary endosperm cell divides repeatedly into triploid endosperm tissue, filled with reserve food for the embryo. In the most common type, the PEN gives free nuclei first (free-nuclear endosperm), then walls form (cellular endosperm). Tender coconut water is free-nuclear endosperm (thousands of nuclei); the white kernel is cellular endosperm.

Endosperm may be fully consumed before seed maturity (pea, groundnut, beans) or persist in the seed (castor, coconut) and be used during germination.

The embryo develops at the micropylar end, where the zygote lies. Most zygotes divide only after some endosperm has formed, to assure nutrition. Embryogeny is similar in monocots and dicots.

The seed

The seed, the final product of sexual reproduction, is often called a fertilised ovule. It has seed coat(s), cotyledon(s) and an embryo axis. Integuments harden into seed coats; the micropyle stays as a pore letting in O2 and water. Seeds dry to 10–15% moisture by mass and may enter dormancy, or germinate if moisture, oxygen and temperature are suitable.

RememberAdvantages of seeds: pollination and fertilisation are independent of water; better dispersal; food reserves for seedlings; a hard coat to protect the embryo; new genetic combinations. Dehydration and dormancy let seeds be stored, the basis of agriculture.
Topic 9

Apomixis and polyembryony

Apomixis is producing seeds without fertilisation, seen in some Asteraceae and grasses. It is a form of asexual reproduction that mimics sexual reproduction.

  • A diploid egg forms without reduction division and develops into an embryo without fertilisation.
  • More often, as in many Citrus and mango varieties, nucellar cells around the embryo sac divide, protrude into it and become embryos.

More than one embryo in a seed is polyembryony. Squeeze an orange seed and you can see several embryos of different sizes.

NEET trapFruit without fertilisation is parthenocarpy; seed without fertilisation is apomixis. Nucellar embryos come from diploid maternal tissue, so they are genetically like the parent.
RememberHybrid seed must be bought every year because the progeny segregate. If hybrids were made apomictic, there would be no segregation and farmers could reuse their seed. Hence the research on apomixis genes for the hybrid seed industry.
