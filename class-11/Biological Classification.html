Topic 1

How classification systems changed

Early classification was instinctive, based on our use of organisms for food, shelter and clothing. Aristotle made the earliest attempt at a scientific basis: he used simple morphological characters to classify plants into trees, shrubs and herbs, and divided animals into those with red blood and those without.

In Linnaeus' time a Two Kingdom system (Plantae and Animalia) was developed. It did not distinguish eukaryotes from prokaryotes, unicellular from multicellular organisms, or photosynthetic (green algae) from non-photosynthetic (fungi) organisms, and many organisms fitted neither kingdom. Characters such as cell structure, nature of wall, mode of nutrition, habitat, reproduction and evolutionary relationships were needed too.

R.H. Whittaker (1969) proposed the Five Kingdom Classification: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia. A three-domain system has also been proposed, dividing Monera into two domains and leaving the eukaryotic kingdoms in the third, giving a six kingdom classification.

Topic 2

Kingdom Monera

Bacteria are the sole members of Monera and the most abundant micro-organisms. Hundreds live in a handful of soil; others live in extreme habitats such as hot springs, deserts, snow and deep oceans, or as parasites. By shape they are coccus (spherical), bacillus (rod), vibrium (comma) and spirillum (spiral).

Though structurally simple, bacteria are complex in behaviour and show the most extensive metabolic diversity of any group. Some are autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic); the vast majority are heterotrophs.

Topic 3

Kingdom Protista

All single-celled eukaryotes are placed in Protista, but its boundaries are not well defined: a "photosynthetic protistan" to one biologist may be "a plant" to another. NCERT includes Chrysophytes, Dinoflagellates, Euglenoids, Slime moulds and Protozoans. Protists are primarily aquatic, have a well-defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, some have flagella or cilia, and they reproduce asexually and sexually (cell fusion and zygote formation). The kingdom forms a link with plants, animals and fungi.

Topic 4

Kingdom Fungi

Fungi are a unique kingdom of heterotrophs, found on moist bread and rotten fruit, as mushrooms and toadstools, as white spots on mustard leaves, as bread- and beer-making yeast, as wheat rust (Puccinia) and as antibiotic sources (Penicillium). They prefer warm and humid places (hence the refrigerator).

Except for unicellular yeasts, fungi are filamentous: thread-like hyphae form a network, the mycelium. Coenocytic hyphae are continuous multinucleate tubes; others have septae (cross walls). Walls are of chitin and polysaccharides. Most are saprophytes; others are parasites or symbionts (with algae as lichens, with roots as mycorrhiza).

Vegetative reproduction: fragmentation, fission, budding. Asexual: conidia, sporangiospores or zoospores. Sexual: oospores, ascospores and basidiospores, produced in fruiting bodies.

Topic 5

Kingdoms Plantae and Animalia

Plantae

All eukaryotic, chlorophyll-containing organisms: algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms. Cells have prominent chloroplasts and a wall mainly of cellulose.

A few are partially heterotrophic: insectivorous bladderwort and Venus fly trap, and the parasite Cuscuta.

The life cycle alternates between a diploid sporophytic and a haploid gametophytic phase: alternation of generation. The lengths of the phases, and whether each is free-living or dependent, vary among groups (Chapter 3).

Animalia

Heterotrophic, eukaryotic, multicellular organisms whose cells lack cell walls. They depend directly or indirectly on plants for food, digest food in an internal cavity and store reserves as glycogen or fat.

Nutrition is holozoic (by ingestion). They follow a definite growth pattern to a definite shape and size; higher forms have elaborate sensory and neuromotor mechanisms; most can move.

Sexual reproduction is by copulation of male and female followed by embryological development (Chapter 4).

Topic 6

Viruses, viroids, prions and lichens

Whittaker's system has no place for lichens or acellular agents like viruses, viroids and prions. Viruses are not considered truly "living" if living means having a cell structure. They are non-cellular, have an inert crystalline structure outside the living cell, and once inside a cell take over its machinery to replicate, killing the host. Virus means venom or poisonous fluid.

A virus is a nucleoprotein: a protein coat plus genetic material that is either RNA or DNA, never both, and the genetic material is infectious. Plant viruses generally have single-stranded RNA; animal viruses have single- or double-stranded RNA or double-stranded DNA; bacteriophages usually have double-stranded DNA. The coat, the capsid, is made of subunits called capsomeres in helical or polyhedral arrangements.

RememberLichens are symbiotic (mutually useful) associations of algae and fungi. The algal partner, the phycobiont, is autotrophic and prepares food; the fungal partner, the mycobiont, is heterotrophic and provides shelter and absorbs minerals and water. Lichens are very good pollution indicators: they do not grow in polluted areas.
