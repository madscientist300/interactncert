Topic 1

The cell cycle

All cells reproduce by dividing into two. During division, DNA replication and cell growth also take place, and all three must be coordinated so that each daughter cell gets an intact genome. The cell cycle is the sequence of events by which a cell duplicates its genome, synthesises the other constituents of the cell and eventually divides into two daughter cells.

Cell growth (cytoplasmic increase) is continuous, but DNA synthesis occurs only in one specific stage. These events are under genetic control.

Human cells in culture divide about once every 24 hours; yeast can complete the cycle in about 90 minutes. The cycle has two basic phases: interphase and the M phase (mitosis phase). Of a 24-hour human cycle, cell division proper lasts only about an hour; interphase takes more than 95% of the time.

RememberIn animals, mitosis is seen only in diploid somatic cells, with exceptions such as male honey bees, whose haploid cells divide by mitosis. Plants show mitosis in both haploid and diploid cells (for example, the haploid gametophytes in alternation of generations).
Topic 2

M phase: mitosis

The M phase is the most dramatic period of the cycle: virtually every component of the cell is reorganised. Because the parent and progeny cells have the same number of chromosomes, mitosis is called equational division. It starts with nuclear division (karyokinesis, separation of daughter chromosomes) and usually ends with division of the cytoplasm (cytokinesis).

Karyokinesis is divided into four stages for convenience: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase. Division is a progressive process, so very clear-cut lines cannot be drawn between stages.

NEET trapThe start of metaphase is marked by the complete disintegration of the nuclear envelope, not the start of condensation. Chromosome morphology is studied at metaphase, because condensation is complete then.
Topic 3

Cytokinesis and the significance of mitosis

Karyokinesis segregates the duplicated chromosomes into daughter nuclei; cytokinesis separates the cytoplasm, completing cell division. During it, organelles such as mitochondria and plastids are distributed between the two daughter cells.

Significance of mitosis

Mitosis is usually restricted to diploid cells, though in some lower plants and some social insects haploid cells also divide by mitosis. It usually produces diploid daughter cells with identical genetic complement.

  • Growth of multicellular organisms is due to mitosis.
  • Cell growth disturbs the ratio between nucleus and cytoplasm; division restores the nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio.
  • Cell repair: cells of the upper layer of the epidermis, the lining of the gut and blood cells are constantly replaced.
  • Mitosis in meristematic tissues (apical and lateral cambium) gives continuous growth of plants throughout their life.
NEET trapWhen karyokinesis is not followed by cytokinesis, a multinucleate condition arises, forming a syncytium. NCERT's example is the liquid endosperm of coconut.
Topic 4

Meiosis and the long prophase I

Sexual reproduction fuses two gametes, each with a haploid set of chromosomes. Gametes are formed from specialised diploid cells by meiosis, the division that halves the chromosome number. Meiosis ensures the haploid phase of the life cycle; fertilisation restores the diploid phase. It occurs during gametogenesis in plants and animals.

Key features of meiosis:

  • Two sequential cycles of nuclear and cell division (meiosis I and II) but only one cycle of DNA replication.
  • Meiosis I begins after the parental chromosomes have replicated into identical sister chromatids in the S phase.
  • Pairing of homologous chromosomes and recombination between non-sister chromatids.
  • Four haploid cells at the end of meiosis II.

Prophase I is longer and more complex than mitotic prophase, and is subdivided into five stages: Leptotene, Zygotene, Pachytene, Diplotene, Diakinesis.

Topic 5

Meiosis I and meiosis II

Each meiotic division has four phases: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase, numbered I or II. Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes; meiosis II separates sister chromatids and resembles a normal mitosis.

NEET trapInterkinesis is the stage between the two meiotic divisions. It is generally short-lived and has no DNA replication. In telophase I, chromosomes may disperse a little but never reach the extended state of an interphase nucleus.
RememberSignificance of meiosis: it conserves the specific chromosome number of each species across generations in sexually reproducing organisms, even though it halves the number. It also increases genetic variability from one generation to the next, and variations are very important for evolution.
