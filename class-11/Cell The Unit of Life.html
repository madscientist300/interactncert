Topic 1

What is a cell, and cell theory

Unicellular organisms can (i) exist independently and (ii) perform the essential functions of life. Anything less than a complete cell cannot live independently. So the cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of all living organisms.

Anton von Leeuwenhoek first saw and described a live cell. Robert Brown later discovered the nucleus. The microscope, and later the electron microscope, revealed the structural details.

Matthias Schleiden (1838), a German botanist, found that all plants are made of different kinds of cells forming tissues. Theodore Schwann (1839), a zoologist, found that animal cells have a thin outer layer, today's plasma membrane, and concluded that the cell wall is unique to plant cells. He proposed that bodies of animals and plants are made of cells and products of cells. Together they formulated the cell theory, but it did not explain how new cells form. Rudolf Virchow (1855) explained that cells divide and new cells come from pre-existing cells (Omnis cellula-e cellula), giving the theory its final shape.

NEET trapRobert Brown discovered the nucleus, not the cell (NCERT exercise 1). NCERT describes Schwann as British; he was in fact German, but exam questions follow the NCERT wording.

Cells with membrane-bound nuclei are eukaryotic; cells without are prokaryotic. In both, a semi-fluid cytoplasm fills the cell and is the main arena of cellular activity. Eukaryotic cells also have membrane-bound organelles; prokaryotes do not. Ribosomes are non-membrane-bound and found in all cells. Animal cells also have the non-membrane-bound centrosome, which helps in cell division.

Cells vary in shape (disc-like, polygonal, columnar, cuboid, thread-like, irregular), and shape can vary with function: round, biconcave RBCs; amoeboid WBCs; long, branched nerve cells; long, narrow columnar epithelial cells; elongated tracheids; round or oval mesophyll cells.

Topic 2

Prokaryotic cells

Prokaryotes are bacteria, blue-green algae, mycoplasma and PPLO (Pleuro Pneumonia Like Organisms). They are generally smaller and multiply more rapidly than eukaryotic cells. All have a cell wall around the cell membrane except mycoplasma. There is no well-defined nucleus: the genetic material is naked, not enveloped by a nuclear membrane. Apart from ribosomes, no eukaryote-like organelles are present.

Topic 3

Eukaryotic cells: plant vs animal

Eukaryotes include all protists, plants, animals and fungi. Their cytoplasm is extensively compartmentalised by membrane-bound organelles; they have an organised nucleus with a nuclear envelope, complex locomotory and cytoskeletal structures, and chromosomes. Plant cells have cell walls, plastids and a large central vacuole; animal cells have centrioles, absent in almost all plant cells.

Topic 4

Cell membrane and cell wall

Chemical studies, especially on human RBCs, showed the membrane is mainly lipids and proteins. The major lipids are phospholipids in a bilayer, with polar heads outward and hydrophobic tails inward, so the nonpolar tails are protected from water. The membrane also has cholesterol, proteins and carbohydrates. The RBC membrane is about 52% protein and 40% lipid.

By ease of extraction, proteins are peripheral (on the surface) or integral (partly or totally buried). The fluid mosaic model of Singer and Nicolson (1972) says the quasi-fluid lipid lets proteins move laterally; this ability is the membrane's fluidity, important for cell growth, intercellular junctions, secretion, endocytosis and cell division.

The cell wall

A non-living, rigid outer covering of the plasma membrane in fungi and plants. It gives shape, protects against mechanical damage and infection, helps cell-to-cell interaction and is a barrier to undesirable macromolecules.

Topic 5

The endomembrane system

The ER, Golgi complex, lysosomes and vacuoles are studied together as the endomembrane system because their functions are coordinated. Mitochondria, chloroplasts and peroxisomes are not part of it, since their functions are not coordinated with these.

Topic 7

Cytoskeleton, cilia, flagella and centrioles

The cytoskeleton is a network of proteinaceous filaments: microtubules, microfilaments and intermediate filaments. It gives mechanical support, motility and maintains cell shape.

Cilia are small and work like oars, moving the cell or the fluid around it. Flagella are longer and move the cell. Bacterial flagella are structurally different from eukaryotic ones. Both cilia and flagella are covered by plasma membrane and emerge from centriole-like basal bodies. The centrosome usually has two centrioles lying perpendicular to each other, surrounded by amorphous pericentriolar material.

Topic 8

Nucleus, chromosomes and microbodies

The interphase nucleus has extended nucleoprotein fibres, the chromatin, a nuclear matrix and one or more nucleoli. The nuclear envelope is two parallel membranes with a perinuclear space (10–50 nm) between them. The outer membrane is continuous with the ER and bears ribosomes. Nuclear pores, formed by fusion of the two membranes, let RNA and protein move both ways (exercise 11).

The nucleolus is not membrane-bound and is the site of active rRNA synthesis; cells making a lot of protein have larger, more numerous nucleoli. Chromatin has DNA, histones (basic proteins), non-histone proteins and RNA. A human cell has about 2 m of DNA in 46 chromosomes (23 pairs). Some mature cells lack a nucleus: erythrocytes of many mammals and sieve tube cells.

Every chromosome, visible only in dividing cells, has a primary constriction, the centromere, which holds the two chromatids, with disc-shaped kinetochores on its sides.
