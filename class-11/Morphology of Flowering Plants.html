Topic 1

The root

Pull out any weed and you see roots, stems and leaves. The underground part is the root system; the part above ground is the shoot system. The root system absorbs water and minerals, gives anchorage, stores reserve food and synthesises plant growth regulators.

Topic 2

The stem and the leaf

The stem is the ascending part of the axis bearing branches, leaves, flowers and fruits. It develops from the plumule. It has nodes (where leaves are borne) and internodes (between nodes), and bears terminal or axillary buds. It is green when young and later often woody and dark brown. It spreads out branches, conducts water, minerals and photosynthates, and some stems store food, support, protect or propagate vegetatively.

The leaf is a lateral, generally flattened structure borne at the node, with a bud in its axil that later forms a branch. Leaves originate from the shoot apical meristem in acropetal order and are the most important vegetative organs for photosynthesis.

NEET trapA bud is present in the axil of the petiole of both simple and compound leaves, but not in the axil of leaflets. That is how you tell a compound leaf from a twig of simple leaves. Topic 3

The inflorescence

A flower is a modified shoot: the shoot apical meristem changes to a floral meristem, internodes do not elongate and the axis condenses; the apex produces floral appendages at successive nodes instead of leaves. A shoot tip that becomes a flower is always solitary. The arrangement of flowers on the floral axis is the inflorescence. The two major types depend on whether the apex becomes a flower or keeps growing.

Topic 4

The flower

The flower is the reproductive unit of angiosperms. A typical flower has four whorls on the swollen end of the stalk (pedicel), the thalamus or receptacle: calyx and corolla (accessory organs), androecium and gynoecium (reproductive organs). When calyx and corolla are not distinct, as in lily, they form a perianth. With both androecium and gynoecium the flower is bisexual; with only one, unisexual. Flowers are trimerous, tetramerous or pentamerous when parts are in multiples of 3, 4 or 5, and bracteate or ebracteate depending on whether a bract (reduced leaf at the base of the pedicel) is present.

Topic 5

The fruit and the seed

The fruit is a characteristic feature of flowering plants: a mature or ripened ovary, developed after fertilisation. A fruit formed without fertilisation is parthenocarpic. It has a wall, the pericarp, and seeds. A thick, fleshy pericarp is differentiated into epicarp, mesocarp and endocarp.

The ovules develop into seeds after fertilisation. A seed has a seed coat and an embryo (radicle, embryonal axis and one cotyledon, as in wheat and maize, or two, as in gram and pea).

Topic 6

Semi-technical description and Solanaceae

A flowering plant is described briefly, in simple scientific language and in a fixed sequence: habit, vegetative characters (root, stem, leaves), then floral characters (inflorescence and flower parts), followed by a floral diagram and a floral formula.

A floral diagram shows the number of parts, their arrangement and their relations. The mother axis is a dot at the top; calyx, corolla, androecium and gynoecium are drawn as successive whorls, calyx outermost and gynoecium in the centre. The floral formula also shows cohesion (fusion within a whorl, shown by brackets) and adhesion (fusion between whorls, shown by a line above the symbols).
