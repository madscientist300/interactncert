Topic 1

What growth is, and where it happens

Development of a mature plant from a zygote follows a precise, highly ordered succession of events. The first step is seed germination, which happens only when conditions are favourable; otherwise the seed enters a period of suspended growth or rest.

Growth is an irreversible permanent increase in size of an organ, its parts, or even a single cell. It is accompanied by metabolic processes, both anabolic and catabolic, that use energy. Expansion of a leaf is growth. A piece of wood swelling in water is not, because the change is reversible and not due to living activity.

Plant growth is indeterminate

Plants keep the capacity for unlimited growth throughout life because of meristems, whose cells divide and self-perpetuate. Their products soon lose the capacity to divide and form the plant body. New cells always being added by meristems is called the open form of growth.

  • Root and shoot apical meristems: primary growth, elongation along the axis.
  • Lateral meristems (vascular cambium, cork cambium) in dicots and gymnosperms: appear later and increase girth. This is secondary growth.

Growth is measurable

At the cellular level growth is mainly an increase in protoplasm. Since that is hard to measure directly, we measure something proportional to it: fresh weight, dry weight, length, area, volume and cell number.

Topic 2

Phases of growth

The period of growth is divided into three phases: meristematic, elongation and maturation. They are easiest to see at a root tip, where they lie one behind the other. Using the parallel line technique on a root, the zones just behind the apex (A to D in NCERT Fig 13.3) are found to have elongated the most.

Topic 3

Growth rates and the sigmoid curve

Increased growth per unit time is the growth rate. It may be arithmetic or geometric.

  • Arithmetic: after mitosis, only one daughter cell keeps dividing; the other differentiates and matures. A root elongating at a constant rate is the simplest example. Length plotted against time gives a straight line: Lt = L0 + rt.
  • Geometric: both daughter cells keep dividing. W1 = W0ert, where r is the relative growth rate, also called the efficiency index. So the final size W1 depends on the initial size W0.

The sigmoid (S-shaped) growth curve

In most systems growth is slow at first (lag phase), then rapid at an exponential rate (log or exponential phase). With limited nutrients it slows down to a stationary phase. A plot of growth against time gives a sigmoid or S-curve, typical of living organisms in a natural environment and of all cells, tissues and organs of a plant.

Absolute vs relative growth rate

Absolute growth rate: total growth per unit time. Relative growth rate: growth per unit time on a common basis, such as per unit initial parameter. In NCERT Fig 13.7, leaves A (5 cm²) and B (50 cm²) both grow by 5 cm². Their absolute growth is equal, but A's relative growth is much higher.

Topic 4

Differentiation, dedifferentiation, redifferentiation

Cells from root and shoot apical meristems and cambium mature to perform specific functions. This is differentiation. To form a tracheary element, for example, cells lose their protoplasm and develop strong, elastic, lignocellulosic secondary walls to carry water long distances under extreme tension.

Living differentiated cells that have lost the capacity to divide can regain it under certain conditions: dedifferentiation. The new meristems produce cells that again lose the capacity to divide and mature: redifferentiation.

RememberDedifferentiation examples: interfascicular cambium and cork cambium formed from fully differentiated parenchyma cells. Parenchyma made to divide in plant tissue culture forms callus, again by dedifferentiation.
NEET trapDifferentiation in plants is also open: cells from the same meristem mature differently depending on position. Cells pushed away from the root apical meristem become root-cap cells; those pushed to the periphery become epidermis.
Topic 5

Development and plasticity

Development includes all the changes an organism goes through in its life cycle, from germination of the seed to senescence. It is the sum of growth and differentiation, and is controlled by intrinsic factors (intracellular genetic factors and intercellular chemicals such as PGRs) and extrinsic factors (light, temperature, water, oxygen, nutrition).

Plasticity and heterophylly

Plants follow different pathways in response to the environment or phase of life, forming different kinds of structures. This ability is plasticity. Different leaf shapes on one plant, heterophylly, is an example.

Phase of lifeCotton, coriander, larkspurLeaves of the juvenile plant differ in shape from those of the mature plant
EnvironmentButtercupLeaves produced in air differ from those produced in water
Topic 6

Plant growth regulators: nature and discovery

Plant growth regulators (PGRs) are small, simple molecules of diverse chemical nature. They are also called plant growth substances, plant hormones or phytohormones.

Chemical natureExample
Indole compoundIndole-3-acetic acid (IAA)
Adenine derivativeN⁶-furfurylamino purine (kinetin)
Carotenoid derivativeAbscisic acid (ABA)
TerpeneGibberellic acid (GA₃)
GasEthylene (C₂H₄)

Promoters: cell division, cell enlargement, pattern formation, tropic growth, flowering, fruiting, seed formation. Auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins.

Inhibitors: responses to wounds and to biotic and abiotic stress; dormancy and abscission. Abscisic acid.

NEET trapEthylene could fit either group, but it is largely an inhibitor of growth activities.

The discovery of each of the five major groups was accidental. It began with Charles Darwin and his son Francis Darwin, who saw that the coleoptiles of canary grass grew towards unilateral light (phototropism).

Landmark discoveries

AuxinCharles & Francis Darwin

Coleoptiles of canary grass bend towards light; the tip is the site of a transmittable influence.

AuxinF.W. Went

Isolated auxin from tips of coleoptiles of oat seedlings.

1926 · GibberellinE. Kurosawa

Sterile filtrates of Gibberella fujikuroi caused bakanae (foolish seedling) disease of rice. The active substance: gibberellic acid.

1955 · CytokininF. Skoog; Miller et al.

Tobacco stem callus grew with auxin plus vascular extract, yeast extract, coconut milk or DNA. Miller et al. crystallised the active substance, kinetin.

Mid-1960s · ABAThree independent groups

Inhibitor-B, abscission II and dormin proved chemically identical: abscisic acid.

1910 · EthyleneH.H. Cousins

A volatile substance from ripened oranges hastened ripening of stored unripe bananas: ethylene.

PGRs work together

For every phase of growth, differentiation and development, some PGR has a role. Roles may be complementary or antagonistic, individualistic or synergistic. Several PGRs interact in events such as dormancy in seeds and buds, abscission, senescence and apical dominance. In most situations ABA acts as an antagonist to GAs.

PGRs are only one kind of intrinsic control. With genomic control and extrinsic factors they govern growth. Many extrinsic factors, such as temperature and light, act via PGRs in events like vernalisation, flowering, dormancy, seed germination and plant movements.
