Topic 1

How plants have been classified

The previous chapter used Whittaker's (1969) Five Kingdom classification: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Animalia and Plantae. Our idea of the plant kingdom has changed over time. Fungi and walled members of Monera and Protista, once placed in Plantae, are now excluded.

Classification systems for plants have moved from a few visible features to evolutionary relationships.

Topic 2

Algae: general features and reproduction

Algae are chlorophyll-bearing, simple, thalloid, autotrophic and largely aquatic (fresh water and marine) organisms. They also grow on moist stones, soil and wood, with fungi (lichen) and on animals (e.g. on the sloth bear).

Form and size vary greatly: colonial (Volvox), filamentous (Ulothrix, Spirogyra), and massive marine forms such as kelps.

  • Vegetative: fragmentation; each fragment grows into a thallus.
  • Asexual: spores, most commonly zoospores, which are flagellated (motile).
  • Sexual: fusion of two gametes, of three kinds below.

Why algae matter

Half of CO2 fixationAt least half of all CO2 fixation on earth is by algae. They raise dissolved O2 and are primary producers of aquatic food cycles.
FoodAbout 70 marine species are eaten, including Porphyra, Laminaria and Sargassum. Chlorella, rich in protein, is a food supplement even for space travellers.
HydrocolloidsWater-holding substances: algin from brown algae, carrageen from red algae.
AgarFrom Gelidium and Gracilaria. Used to grow microbes and in ice-creams and jellies.

Topic 4

Bryophytes: liverworts and mosses

Bryophytes (mosses and liverworts) grow in moist, shaded areas in the hills. They are the amphibians of the plant kingdom: they live in soil but need water for sexual reproduction. They help in plant succession on bare rocks and soil.

The body is more differentiated than in algae: thallus-like, prostrate or erect, attached by unicellular or multicellular rhizoids. They have no true roots, stem or leaves, only root-like, stem-like and leaf-like structures. The main plant body is haploid and makes gametes, so it is the gametophyte.

Sex organs are multicellular. The antheridium makes biflagellate antherozoids; the flask-shaped archegonium makes a single egg. The zygote does not undergo meiosis at once; it grows into a multicellular sporophyte that is not free-living but attached to, and nourished by, the gametophyte.

Topic 5

Pteridophytes: the first vascular land plants

Pteridophytes include horsetails and ferns, used as medicine, soil-binders and ornamentals. They are the first terrestrial plants with vascular tissues, xylem and phloem. They grow in cool, damp, shady places; some do well in sandy soil.

The main plant body is a sporophyte with true root, stem and leaves. Leaves are small microphylls (Selaginella) or large macrophylls (ferns). Sporangia are borne on leaf-like sporophylls, which may form compact strobili or cones (Selaginella, Equisetum).

NEET trapRetaining the female gametophyte on the sporophyte, with the embryo developing inside it, is a precursor to the seed habit, an important step in evolution. Topic 6

Gymnosperms: naked seeds

In gymnosperms (gymnos, naked; sperma, seeds) the ovules are not enclosed by an ovary wall, before or after fertilisation, so the seeds are naked. They are medium to tall trees and shrubs; the giant redwood Sequoia is one of the tallest tree species.

Body

  • Roots generally tap roots. Pinus: fungal association, mycorrhiza. Cycas: coralloid roots with N2-fixing cyanobacteria.
  • Stems unbranched (Cycas) or branched (Pinus, Cedrus).
  • Leaves simple or compound; Cycas pinnate leaves persist for a few years. Conifer needle leaves, thick cuticle and sunken stomata reduce water loss.

Reproduction

  • Heterosporous: haploid microspores and megaspores, in sporangia on sporophylls arranged spirally into strobili (cones).
  • Male strobili: microsporophylls with microsporangia. Female strobili: megasporophylls with ovules.
  • Pinus: male and female cones on the same tree. Cycas: male cones and megasporophylls on different trees.

NEET trapUnlike bryophytes and pteridophytes, gymnosperm gametophytes have no independent free-living existence; they stay within sporangia on the sporophyte. The male gametophyte is highly reduced to a few cells: the pollen grain. The female gametophyte bears two or more archegonia.
Topic 7

Angiosperms, and the groups compared

In angiosperms (flowering plants), pollen grains and ovules develop in specialised structures called flowers, and seeds are enclosed in fruits. They are an exceptionally large group, from the smallest, Wolffia, to Eucalyptus trees over 100 metres tall. They give food, fodder, fuel and medicines. They are divided into two classes: dicotyledons and monocotyledons.
