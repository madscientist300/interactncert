Topic 1

Do plants breathe?

All living organisms need energy for absorption, transport, movement, reproduction and even breathing. This energy comes from the oxidation of food. Green plants and cyanobacteria make their own food by photosynthesis, but only cells with chloroplasts can do this. All other cells, such as those of roots and woody stems, need food translocated to them for oxidation. Ultimately, all food that is respired comes from photosynthesis.

The breaking of C–C bonds of complex compounds through oxidation within the cell, releasing a considerable amount of energy, is called respiration. The compounds oxidised are respiratory substrates. Usually these are carbohydrates; proteins, fats and even organic acids can be used in some plants under certain conditions.

The energy is not released in one step. It comes out in a series of slow, step-wise, enzyme-controlled reactions and is trapped as ATP, the energy currency of the cell. The carbon skeletons produced are used as precursors to make other molecules.

Why plants need no respiratory organs

Plants require O2 and give out CO2, but exchange gases through stomata and lenticels. Three reasons:

  • Each plant part handles its own gas exchange. Very little gas is transported from one part to another.
  • Demand is low. Roots, stems and leaves respire far more slowly than animals. Large volumes are exchanged only in photosynthesis, when O2 is released inside the cell anyway.
  • Diffusion distances are short. Living cells lie close to the surface. In stems they form thin layers beneath the bark; interior cells are dead. Loosely packed parenchyma gives a network of air spaces.

Where respiration happens

Photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts. Breakdown of food to release energy happens in the cytoplasm and in the mitochondria (mitochondria only in eukaryotes).

NEET trapEnergy released by oxidation is not used directly. It is used to make ATP, which is broken down wherever energy is needed.

Topic 2

Glycolysis: glucose to two pyruvic acid

All organisms keep the enzymes to partially oxidise glucose without oxygen. The breakdown of glucose to pyruvic acid is glycolysis (Greek glycos, sugar; lysis, splitting). Its scheme was given by Gustav Embden, Otto Meyerhof and J. Parnas, so it is called the EMP pathway.

Glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm of all living organisms. In anaerobic organisms it is the only process in respiration. In plants the glucose comes from sucrose, the end product of photosynthesis, or from stored carbohydrates. The enzyme invertase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, and both enter the pathway. Glycolysis is a chain of ten reactions, each controlled by a different enzyme.

NEET trapThere is only one step that forms NADH + H+: PGAL → BPGA. Two redox equivalents (two H atoms) go from PGAL to NAD+. Per glucose this gives 2 NADH + H+.

The fate of pyruvic acid depends on the cell's needs. There are three ways to handle it: lactic acid fermentation, alcoholic fermentation and aerobic respiration (Krebs' cycle), which needs O2.

Topic 3

Fermentation: incomplete oxidation without O2

Fermentation takes place under anaerobic conditions in many prokaryotes, unicellular eukaryotes and germinating seeds. In yeast, pyruvic acid is converted to CO2 and ethanol by pyruvic acid decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase. Some bacteria make lactic acid instead. Animal muscle cells do the same during exercise when O2 is inadequate, using lactate dehydrogenase.

In both, the reducing agent is NADH + H+, which is reoxidised to NAD+. This recycles NAD+ so that glycolysis can continue.

Topic 4

Aerobic respiration: link reaction and Krebs' cycle

Aerobic respiration is the complete oxidation of organic substances in the presence of O2, releasing CO2, water and a large amount of energy. In eukaryotes it happens in the mitochondria, so pyruvate is first transported there from the cytoplasm. Two crucial events follow:

  • Complete oxidation of pyruvate by stepwise removal of all hydrogen atoms, leaving three CO2. This happens in the matrix.
  • Passing the electrons removed with the hydrogen to molecular O2, with ATP synthesis. This happens on the inner mitochondrial membrane.

The link reaction: oxidative decarboxylation

In the matrix, pyruvate undergoes oxidative decarboxylation catalysed by pyruvic dehydrogenase. This needs several coenzymes, including NAD+ and coenzyme A, and Mg2+.

Pyruvic acid + CoA + NAD+ —(Mg2+, pyruvate dehydrogenase)→ Acetyl CoA + CO2 + NADH + H+

Two pyruvic acid come from one glucose, so this step makes 2 NADH per glucose. Acetyl CoA then enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, called Krebs' cycle after Hans Krebs, who first worked it out.

NEET trapUp to the end of the TCA cycle, one glucose has given 8 NADH + H+ (2 link + 6 cycle) and 2 FADH2, but only 2 ATP in the TCA cycle. O2 has not been used yet. GTP formation at succinyl-CoA → succinic acid is substrate-level phosphorylation.

Topic 5

Electron transport system and oxidative phosphorylation

The energy stored in NADH + H+ and FADH2 is released when they are oxidised through the electron transport system (ETS), a chain of carriers in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The electrons pass to O2, forming H2O.

Simplified from NCERT Fig 12.4. Electron path shown dashed; proton pumping shown in red.

ATP synthase (complex V)

Complex V has two parts. F1 is the headpiece, a peripheral membrane protein complex that holds the site for making ATP from ADP and Pi. F0 is an integral membrane protein complex that forms the channel through which protons cross the inner membrane.

For each ATP made, 4H+ pass through F0 from the intermembrane space to the matrix, down the electrochemical proton gradient.

Remember1 NADH → 3 ATP. 1 FADH2 → 2 ATP. The number depends on the electron donor.
NEET trapO2 acts only at the terminal stage, as the final hydrogen acceptor, yet it is vital because it drives the whole process by removing hydrogen. In photophosphorylation, light makes the proton gradient; in respiration it is the energy of oxidation–reduction, hence oxidative phosphorylation.
Topic 6

The respiratory balance sheet

We can calculate the net ATP gain per glucose, but it stays a theoretical exercise. It rests on four assumptions:

  • A sequential, orderly pathway: glycolysis, then TCA cycle, then ETS, one substrate forming the next.
  • The NADH made in glycolysis is transferred into the mitochondria and undergoes oxidative phosphorylation.
  • No intermediates are withdrawn to make other compounds.
  • Only glucose is respired; no other substrate enters midway.

In a living cell none of these hold: pathways run at the same time, substrates enter and leave as needed, and ATP is used as it is made. The exercise still shows how efficient the system is: a net gain of 38 ATP per glucose.

Topic 7

The amphibolic pathway

Glucose is the favoured substrate. Other carbohydrates are usually first converted to glucose. Other substrates can be respired too, but they do not enter at the first step:

  • Fats are broken into glycerol and fatty acids. Fatty acids → acetyl CoA; glycerol → PGAL.
  • Proteins are broken down by proteases. Amino acids, after deamination, enter at pyruvate, acetyl CoA or within the Krebs' cycle, depending on their structure.

The same compounds are withdrawn from the pathway when the cell needs to make these substances. For example, acetyl CoA is withdrawn to synthesise fatty acids. Since the respiratory pathway takes part in both catabolism (breakdown) and anabolism (synthesis), it is better called an amphibolic pathway than a catabolic one.

Topic 8

Respiratory quotient

The ratio of the volume of CO2 evolved to the volume of O2 consumed in respiration is the respiratory quotient (RQ), or respiratory ratio. It depends on the type of substrate.

RQ = volume of CO2 evolved / volume of O2 consumed
