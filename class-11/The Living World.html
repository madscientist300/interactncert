Topic 1

Diversity in the living world

Living organisms are found in cold mountains, deciduous forests, oceans, fresh water lakes, deserts and hot springs. Asking "what is life?" hides two questions: a technical one (what is living, as opposed to non-living) and a philosophical one (the purpose of life). Scientists attempt only the first.

Each different kind of plant, animal or organism is a species. The number of known and described species is 1.7–1.8 million. This is biodiversity: the number and types of organisms on earth. New organisms are still being identified, both in new areas and old ones.

Local names vary from place to place, even within one country. To avoid confusion, names must be standardised so an organism has the same name all over the world.

RememberThe wider the area you observe, the greater the range and variety of organisms you see. A dense forest shows far more kinds than a garden.
Topic 2

Identification and nomenclature

Describing an organism correctly so we know which organism a name belongs to is identification. Standardising the naming so the organism has the same name all over the world is nomenclature. Naming is only possible after correct identification.

Scientific names follow agreed principles: the International Code for Botanical Nomenclature (ICBN) for plants and the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN) for animals. They make sure each organism has only one name, that a description leads anyone anywhere to the same name, and that the name is not used for any other organism.

Each name has two parts, the generic name and the specific epithet: binomial nomenclature, given by Carolus Linnaeus.

Universal rules of nomenclature

  1. Biological names are generally in Latin and written in italics. They are Latinised or derived from Latin whatever their origin.
  2. The first word is the genus; the second is the specific epithet.
  3. When handwritten, both words are separately underlined; when printed, they are in italics, to show Latin origin.
  4. The genus starts with a capital letter; the specific epithet with a small letter.

The author's name comes after the specific epithet, in abbreviated form: Mangifera indica Linn. means the species was first described by Linnaeus.

Topic 3

Classification, taxonomy and systematics

It is nearly impossible to study every organism, so we group them. Classification is grouping anything into convenient categories based on easily observable characters. Hearing "mammals", we picture animals with external ears and body hair. These categories are called taxa, and taxa can be at very different levels.

Classifying organisms into taxa based on characteristics is taxonomy. Modern taxonomy is based on external and internal structure, cell structure, development and ecological information. The earliest classifications were based on the uses of organisms for food, clothing and shelter.

Topic 4

Taxonomic categories and the hierarchy

Classification is a hierarchy of steps; each step is a rank or category. Because each is part of the overall arrangement, it is a taxonomic category, and all the categories together make the taxonomic hierarchy. Each category, a unit of classification, is called a taxon (plural taxa). Taxa are distinct biological entities, not merely morphological aggregates. Insects, for example, share three pairs of jointed legs.

Topic 5

How closely are two organisms related?

Two organisms are more closely related the lower the category they share. Lion and tiger share a genus; lion and cat only a family; cat and dog only an order.

People to remember

Binomial nomenclatureCarolus Linnaeus

Gave the two-word naming system; titled his work Systema Naturae. "Linn." after a name marks species he first described.

1904–2004Ernst Mayr

Harvard evolutionary biologist called "the Darwin of the 20th century"; pioneered the accepted definition of a biological species. Won the Balzan (1983), International Prize for Biology (1994) and Crafoord (1999) prizes.

CodesICBN and ICZN

International codes of nomenclature for plants and for animals.
