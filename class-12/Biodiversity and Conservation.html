Topic 1

What is biodiversity?

Diversity (heterogeneity) exists at every level of biological organisation, from macromolecules within cells to biomes. Biodiversity, the term popularised by the sociobiologist Edward Wilson, describes the combined diversity at all these levels. It took millions of years of evolution to accumulate, and could be lost in less than two centuries at present rates of species loss.

Topic 2

How many species on Earth, and in India?

According to the IUCN (2004), slightly more than 1.5 million plant and animal species have been described. How many remain undescribed is unclear; species inventories are more complete in temperate than in tropical countries, and most undiscovered species are in the tropics. So biologists compare temperate–tropical species richness of an exhaustively studied insect group and extrapolate the ratio to other groups (exercise 2). Extreme estimates reach 20 to 50 million; Robert May's conservative, scientifically sound estimate is about 7 million.

India: about 45,000 recorded plant species and twice as many animals. 22% = 1.5 million recorded ÷ May's 7 million.

RememberMore than 70% of recorded species are animals; plants (algae, fungi, bryophytes, gymnosperms, angiosperms) are no more than 22%. Insects are more than 70% of animals: 7 of every 10 animals. Fungi outnumber fishes, amphibians, reptiles and mammals combined.
NEET trapProkaryotes are left out: conventional taxonomic methods do not suit microbes and many are not culturable. By biochemical or molecular criteria their diversity alone might run into millions. India has 2.4% of world land but 8.1% of species diversity. "Nature's biological library is burning" before we catalogue its books.
Topic 3

Patterns of biodiversity

Latitudinal gradient: birds from the equator to the pole

Fig 4 · interactive

The four labelled points are NCERT's; the curve joining them and India's plotted latitude are illustrative.

Species diversity generally decreases from the equator to the poles; with few exceptions the tropics (23.5° N to 23.5° S) hold more species. A tropical forest in Ecuador has up to 10 times as many vascular plant species as an equal temperate forest in the US Midwest. The Amazonian rain forest has the greatest biodiversity on Earth: more than 40,000 plants, 3,000 fishes, 1,300 birds, 427 mammals, 427 amphibians, 378 reptiles and 1,25,000 invertebrates, and perhaps two million undiscovered insects.

Why are the tropics so rich? (exercise 3)Hypothesis
More evolutionary timeSpeciation is a function of time. Temperate regions suffered frequent glaciations; tropics stayed relatively undisturbed for millions of years.
Constant environmentLess seasonal, more constant and predictable: promotes niche specialisation, hence more species.
More solar energyHigher productivity, which might indirectly support greater diversity.

NEET trapAlexander von Humboldt observed that species richness increases with explored area, but only up to a limit. The curve is a rectangular hyperbola; on a log scale it becomes a straight line, log S = log C + Z log A (Z = slope, the regression coefficient; C = Y-intercept). Z = 0.1–0.2 regardless of taxon or region (plants in Britain, birds in California, molluscs in New York state); for whole continents Z is steeper, 0.6–1.2 (frugivorous birds and mammals in tropical forests: 1.15).
Topic 4

Why species diversity matters to the ecosystem

Ecologists long believed that communities with more species are more stable. A stable community should not vary too much in productivity from year to year; it should be resistant or resilient to occasional disturbances; and it should resist invasion by alien species. A definitive answer is still lacking.

Topic 5

Loss of biodiversity

It is doubtful that new species are being added by speciation, but losses are certain, and the finger points at human activities. Human colonisation of tropical Pacific islands led to the extinction of more than 2,000 native bird species. Recent extinctions: dodo (Mauritius), quagga (Africa), thylacine (Australia), Steller's sea cow (Russia), and three tiger subspecies (Bali, Javan, Caspian). 27 species disappeared in the last twenty years. Extinctions are not random: amphibians seem more vulnerable.

Topic 6

Biodiversity conservation

Why conserve?Argument
Narrowly utilitarianDirect economic benefits: food, firewood, fibre, construction material, industrial products (tannins, lubricants, dyes, resins, perfumes), medicines. > 25% of drugs sold worldwide come from plants; 25,000 plant species are used in traditional medicine. Bioprospecting: exploring molecular, genetic and species diversity for products of economic importance.
Broadly utilitarianEcosystem services: the Amazon produces about 20% of Earth's atmospheric oxygen; pollination by bees, bumblebees, birds and bats; intangible aesthetic pleasures (woods, spring flowers, a bulbul's song).
EthicalEvery species has intrinsic value, even with no economic value to us; a moral duty to pass our biological legacy to future generations in good order.

Biodiversity hotspots

Fig 11 · interactive
RememberIndia's in situ network: 14 biosphere reserves, 90 national parks, 448 wildlife sanctuaries. Sacred groves: tracts of forest venerated and totally protected: Khasi and Jaintia Hills (Meghalaya), Aravalli Hills (Rajasthan), Western Ghats of Karnataka and Maharashtra, Sarguja, Chanda and Bastar (Madhya Pradesh). In Meghalaya they are the last refuges of many rare plants (exercise 7).
NEET trapIn situ protects the whole ecosystem: "save the entire forest to save the tiger". Ex situ is for threatened species facing very high risk of extinction in the wild: zoos, botanical gardens, safari parks, cryopreservation of gametes, in vitro fertilisation, tissue culture, seed banks. Some animals extinct in the wild survive only in zoos.

International action

1992Convention on Biological Diversity

"The Earth Summit", Rio de Janeiro: all nations to conserve biodiversity and use its benefits sustainably.

2002World Summit on Sustainable Development

Johannesburg, South Africa: 190 countries pledged a significant reduction in the rate of biodiversity loss by 2010.

2004IUCN Red List

784 extinctions documented in the last 500 years; more than 15,500 species threatened.
