Topic 1

Ecosystem: structure and function

An ecosystem is a functional unit of nature where living organisms interact among themselves and with their physical environment. Sizes range from a small pond to a forest or a sea; many ecologists treat the whole biosphere as one global ecosystem. Two basic categories: terrestrial (forest, grassland, desert) and aquatic (pond, lake, wetland, river, estuary). Crop fields and aquaria are man-made ecosystems.

Biotic and abiotic interactions give each ecosystem a characteristic physical structure. Its two main structural features are species composition (identifying and listing species) and stratification (vertical distribution of species at different levels). The components function as a unit through four aspects: productivity, decomposition, energy flow and nutrient cycling.

Topic 2

Productivity

A constant input of solar energy is the basic requirement for any ecosystem. Primary production is the amount of biomass or organic matter produced per unit area over a time period by plants during photosynthesis, in weight (g m⁻²) or energy (kcal m⁻²). The rate of biomass production is productivity, in g m⁻² yr⁻¹ or kcal m⁻² yr⁻¹, so ecosystems can be compared.

RememberNPP is the biomass available to heterotrophs (herbivores and decomposers). Secondary productivity is the rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers. Primary productivity depends on the plant species, environmental factors, nutrient availability and the plants' photosynthetic capacity, so it varies between ecosystems.
NEET trapAnnual NPP of the biosphere ≈ 170 billion tons (dry weight); oceans, despite covering about 70% of the surface, produce only 55 billion tons. In aquatic ecosystems the limiting factor for productivity is light (exercise 1c), which falls off with depth, along with nutrients.
Topic 3

Decomposition

The earthworm is the farmer's "friend": it breaks down complex organic matter and loosens the soil. Decomposers break complex organic matter into inorganic substances: CO₂, water and nutrients. The raw material is detritus: dead plant remains (leaves, bark, flowers) and dead animal remains, including faecal matter.

RememberHumus: dark, amorphous, highly resistant to microbial action, decomposes extremely slowly; being colloidal it is a reservoir of nutrients. Decomposition is largely oxygen-requiring. Temperature and soil moisture are the most important climatic factors. Warm and moist favours decomposition; low temperature and anaerobiosis inhibit it, so organic matter builds up.
Topic 4

Energy flow

Except for the deep-sea hydrothermal ecosystem, the sun is the only energy source for all ecosystems. Less than 50% of incident solar radiation is photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), and plants capture only 2–10% of the PAR; this small amount sustains the entire living world. Energy flows unidirectionally: sun → producers → consumers. Ecosystems obey the second law of thermodynamics: they need a constant energy supply to build the molecules they need and to counteract the universal tendency towards disorder.

RememberTrophic level: the place an organism occupies in the food chain by its source of food. Producers 1st, herbivores (primary consumers) 2nd, carnivores (secondary consumers) 3rd. Herbivores: insects, birds and mammals on land; molluscs in water. Standing crop: mass of living material at a trophic level at a given time, as biomass or number per unit area; dry weight is more accurate.
NEET trapA trophic level is a functional level, not a species: a sparrow is a primary consumer eating seeds, fruits and peas, and a secondary consumer eating insects and worms. Omnivores such as cockroaches and crows link GFC and DFC into a food web. The number of GFC trophic levels is limited by the 10% law. The second trophic level in a lake is zooplankton (exercise 3).
Topic 5

Ecological pyramids

Food or energy relationships between trophic levels, expressed as number, biomass or energy, form a pyramid: the base is the producers (first trophic level), the apex the top consumer. Calculations must include all organisms at a trophic level. In most ecosystems all three pyramids are upright.

NEET trapPyramid of energy is always upright, never inverted: some energy is lost as heat at every step. Pyramid of biomass in the sea is generally inverted (fish biomass exceeds phytoplankton's: a small, fast-turning standing crop of phytoplankton supports a larger one of zooplankton). Pyramid of numbers is inverted for one big tree with many insects (exercise 1b: tree-dominated ecosystem).
RememberLimitations of ecological pyramids: (1) they ignore the same species belonging to two or more trophic levels; (2) they assume a simple food chain, which almost never exists, and do not accommodate a food web; (3) saprophytes are given no place, though they play a vital role.
