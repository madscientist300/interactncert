Topic 1

Microbes in household products

Lactobacillus and other lactic acid bacteria (LAB) turn milk into curd. As they grow they make acids that coagulate and partially digest milk proteins. A little curd added to fresh milk as an inoculum (starter) carries millions of LAB, which multiply at a suitable temperature. Curd is nutritionally better, with more vitamin B₁₂; in our stomach LAB also check disease-causing microbes.

Topic 2

Microbes in industrial products

Industrial production needs microbes grown in very large vessels called fermentors. Brewer's yeast, the same Saccharomyces cerevisiae used for bread, ferments malted cereals and fruit juices to ethanol.

Antibiotics ("against life" for pathogens, "pro life" for us) are chemicals made by some microbes that kill or retard the growth of other, disease-causing microbes. Penicillin was the first. Antibiotics transformed treatment of plague, whooping cough (kali khansi), diphtheria (gal ghotu) and leprosy (kusht rog).

Topic 3

Microbes in sewage treatment

Municipal waste water, sewage, is mostly human excreta, rich in organic matter and microbes, many of them pathogenic. It cannot go straight into rivers, so sewage treatment plants (STPs) treat it using the heterotrophic microbes naturally present in it, in two stages.

RememberBOD (biochemical oxygen demand): the amount of oxygen that would be consumed if all the organic matter in one litre of water were oxidised by bacteria. It measures the rate of oxygen uptake by microbes, so it is an indirect measure of organic matter. Higher BOD = more polluting.
NEET trapPrimary treatment is physical (filtration, sedimentation). Secondary treatment is biological: aerobic flocs in aeration tanks, then anaerobic bacteria in sludge digesters. The Ministry of Environment and Forests started the Ganga Action Plan and Yamuna Action Plan to build more STPs. No man-made technology yet rivals microbial treatment.
Topic 4

Microbes in production of biogas

Biogas is a mixture of gases, predominantly methane, produced by microbes and usable as fuel. Bacteria that grow anaerobically on cellulosic material produce much methane with CO₂ and H₂: the methanogens, e.g. Methanobacterium. They live in the anaerobic sludge of sewage plants and in the rumen of cattle, where they help break down cellulose. So cattle dung (gobar) is rich in them: gobar gas.

RememberThe plant: a concrete tank 10–15 feet deep fed with dung slurry and bio-wastes; a floating cover rises as gas forms; one outlet pipes gas to houses for cooking and lighting; another removes spent slurry, usable as fertiliser. Built mostly in rural areas. Developed in India mainly by IARI (Indian Agricultural Research Institute) and KVIC (Khadi and Village Industries Commission).
Topic 5

Microbes as biocontrol agents

Biocontrol: biological methods to control plant diseases and pests, instead of toxic insecticides, pesticides and weedicides that pollute soil, ground water and food. The organic farmer believes that biodiversity furthers health: pests are kept at manageable levels by checks and balances, not eradicated, because beneficial predators and parasites depend on them. Knowing the field's life forms, their life cycles, feeding and habitats helps design biocontrol.

Topic 6

Microbes as biofertilisers

Overuse of chemical fertilisers pollutes, so there is pressure to switch to organic farming. Biofertilisers are organisms that enrich the nutrient quality of the soil. Their main sources are bacteria, fungi and cyanobacteria, and many are sold commercially to farmers.

NEET trapMycorrhiza (many Glomus species) supplies phosphorus, not nitrogen, and also gives resistance to root-borne pathogens, tolerance to salinity and drought, and better overall growth. Symbiotic N-fixer: Rhizobium (legume nodules). Free-living N-fixers: Azospirillum, Azotobacter. Cyanobacteria (Anabaena, Nostoc, Oscillatoria) fix N and add organic matter in paddy fields.
