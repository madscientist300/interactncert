Topic 1

The structure and packaging of DNA

DNA is a long polymer of deoxyribonucleotides. A nucleotide has a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA) and a phosphate. Purines: adenine, guanine. Pyrimidines: cytosine, thymine, uracil. Thymine is in DNA; uracil replaces it in RNA.

  • Base + sugar (via N-glycosidic linkage at 1′C) = nucleoside.
  • Nucleoside + phosphate (via phosphoester linkage at 5′C) = nucleotide.
  • Nucleotides join by 3′–5′ phosphodiester linkages. The chain has a free phosphate at the 5′ end and a free 3′-OH at the 3′ end. The backbone is sugar–phosphate; bases project from it.
  • RNA has an extra 2′-OH on every ribose, and uracil instead of thymine (5-methyl uracil).

The double helix was proposed in 1953 by Watson and Crick, using X-ray diffraction data of Maurice Wilkins and Rosalind Franklin and Erwin Chargaff's finding that A/T and G/C ratios are constant and equal to one.

Salient features of the double helix

  • Two polynucleotide chains; sugar–phosphate backbone outside, bases inside.
  • Anti-parallel: one chain 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′.
  • A=T with two H-bonds; G≡C with three. A purine always faces a pyrimidine, keeping the strands an almost uniform distance apart.
  • Right-handed coil. Pitch 3.4 nm, about 10 bp per turn, so 0.34 nm between base pairs.
  • Base pairs stack on one another; with H-bonds this stabilises the helix.

Packaging of the DNA helix

A typical mammalian cell has 6.6 × 10⁹ bp. At 0.34 nm per bp, its DNA is about 2.2 metres long, far longer than a nucleus (about 10⁻⁶ m).

In prokaryotes such as E. coli, negatively charged DNA is held by positively charged proteins in the nucleoid, organised in large loops. In eukaryotes, positively charged histones, rich in lysine and arginine, form a histone octamer. DNA wraps around it to form a nucleosome containing about 200 bp. Nucleosomes are the repeating units of chromatin, seen as beads-on-string under the electron microscope. Higher-level packaging needs non-histone chromosomal (NHC) proteins.

Topic 2

The search for the genetic material

DNA was first identified as an acidic substance in the nucleus by Friedrich Meischer (1869), who called it 'nuclein'. By 1926, work by Mendel, Sutton, Morgan and others had narrowed the search to chromosomes, but which molecule carried the information was still open.

Transforming principle (Griffith, 1928): Streptococcus pneumoniae has an S strain (smooth colonies, polysaccharide mucous coat, virulent) and an R strain (rough colonies, no coat, harmless).

Topic 3

Replication

Watson and Crick proposed that the two strands separate and each acts as a template. Each daughter molecule then has one parental and one new strand: semiconservative replication.

Meselson and Stahl (1958) grew E. coli for many generations in ¹⁵NH₄Cl (¹⁵N is heavy, not radioactive), then moved them to ¹⁴NH₄Cl and separated DNA by density in a CsCl gradient. E. coli divides every 20 minutes.

Blue: heavy ¹⁵N strand. Grey: light ¹⁴N strand. Taylor and colleagues (1958) showed the same in Vicia faba chromosomes using radioactive thymidine.

The main enzyme is DNA-dependent DNA polymerase. E. coli (4.6 × 10⁶ bp) replicates in 18 minutes, about 2000 bp per second, with high accuracy; errors cause mutations. Deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates are both substrates and the energy source.

The strands cannot be separated along their whole length, so replication happens in a small opening, the replication fork. It starts at a definite origin of replication; this is why recombinant DNA work needs a vector, which supplies an origin. In eukaryotes replication happens in the S phase; failure of cell division after replication gives polyploidy.

Topic 4

Transcription

Transcription copies information from one strand of a segment of DNA into RNA. Adenine pairs with uracil instead of thymine. Both strands are not copied because they would give two different proteins from one segment, and the two RNAs would pair into double-stranded RNA that could not be translated.

A transcription unit has a promoter, a structural gene and a terminator. RNA polymerase works only 5′ → 3′, so the strand with 3′ → 5′ polarity is the template strand. The other strand (5′ → 3′), with the same sequence as the RNA except T for U, is called the coding strand, even though it codes for nothing. All references use the coding strand: the promoter is upstream (5′) and the terminator downstream (3′). The promoter is the binding site for RNA polymerase and defines which strand is the template.

Topic 5

The genetic code and mutations

Physicist George Gamow argued that with 4 bases and 20 amino acids, the code must be a triplet (4³ = 64 codons). Har Gobind Khorana's chemical method made RNA with defined bases (homopolymers and copolymers); Marshall Nirenberg's cell-free system finally deciphered the code; Severo Ochoa's enzyme (polynucleotide phosphorylase) polymerised RNA without a template.

Salient features of the code

  • Triplet: 64 codons; 61 code for amino acids, 3 are stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA).
  • Degenerate: some amino acids have more than one codon.
  • Read contiguously, with no punctuation.
  • Nearly universal (UUU = Phe from bacteria to humans); exceptions in mitochondria and some protozoans.
  • AUG has dual function: codes for Met and is the initiator codon.

Mutations

A point mutation in the β-globin gene changes glutamate to valine, causing sickle cell anaemia.

Inserting or deleting one or two bases shifts the reading frame from that point: frameshift mutation. Inserting or deleting three (or multiples) adds or removes whole codons, and the frame stays the same afterwards.

RemembertRNA, the adapter molecule (once called sRNA, soluble RNA), predicted by Crick. It has an anticodon loop complementary to the codon and an amino acid acceptor end. tRNAs are specific for each amino acid; there is a special initiator tRNA and no tRNA for stop codons. Secondary structure: clover-leaf; actual 3-D shape: inverted L.
Topic 6

Translation

Translation polymerises amino acids into a polypeptide in the order set by mRNA, joining them by peptide bonds. The ribosome is the factory: structural RNAs and about 80 proteins, in a large and a small subunit when inactive. A translational unit runs from the start codon (AUG) to a stop codon; untranslated regions (UTRs) at the 5′ and 3′ ends help efficient translation.

Topic 7

Regulation of gene expression: the lac operon

In eukaryotes, expression can be regulated at the transcriptional, processing (splicing), mRNA transport and translational levels. In prokaryotes, control of the rate of transcriptional initiation dominates. Accessory proteins act as activators (positive) or repressors (negative); repressors bind operators next to promoters. Each operon has its own operator and repressor.

The lac operon was worked out by geneticist François Jacob and biochemist Jacques Monod, the first transcriptionally regulated system. A polycistronic structural gene shares a common promoter and regulatory genes: this is an operon (others: trp, ara, his, val).

Topic 8

Human Genome Project and DNA fingerprinting

The Human Genome Project (HGP), launched in 1990 and completed in 2003, was a 13-year mega project coordinated by the US Department of Energy and the National Institute of Health, with the Wellcome Trust (UK) and others. At about US $3 per bp for 3 × 10⁹ bp, the estimated cost was 9 billion US dollars; printed at 1000 letters a page and 1000 pages a book, one cell's sequence would fill 3300 books. It drove the growth of bioinformatics.

Goals

  • Identify all the approximately 20,000–25,000 genes
  • Sequence the 3 billion base pairs
  • Store the data; improve analysis tools
  • Transfer technologies to other sectors
  • Address ethical, legal and social issues (ELSI)

Methods

Expressed Sequence Tags (ESTs): identify genes expressed as RNA. Or sequence everything and later assign functions: sequence annotation. DNA was fragmented and cloned in BAC and YAC vectors, sequenced by automated sequencers using Frederick Sanger's method, and aligned by computer using overlaps.

Salient features

  • 3164.7 million bp
  • Average gene 3000 bases; largest, dystrophin, 2.4 million bases
  • About 30,000 genes; 99.9% of bases identical in all humans
  • Functions unknown for over 50% of genes
  • Less than 2% codes for proteins; repeats make up a very large part
  • Chromosome 1 has most genes (2968); Y fewest (231)
  • About 1.4 million SNPs ("snips")
  • Chromosome 1 was the last sequenced, in May 2006

DNA fingerprinting

DNA fingerprinting compares individuals at repetitive DNA. In density gradient centrifugation, bulk DNA forms a major peak and the small peaks are satellite DNA (micro-satellites, mini-satellites). These do not code for proteins but show a high degree of polymorphism: a variant at a locus with frequency greater than 0.01 in a population. DNA from any tissue (blood, hair follicle, skin, bone, saliva, sperm) shows the same pattern, and patterns are inherited, so it is used in forensics and paternity testing.

Developed by Alec Jeffreys, using a mini-satellite probe called VNTR (Variable Number of Tandem Repeats), 0.1 to 20 kb in size, in Southern blot hybridisation. Bands differ between individuals except monozygotic twins. With PCR, a single cell's DNA is enough.
