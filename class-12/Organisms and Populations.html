Topic 1

Population attributes

Ecology studies interactions among organisms and between organisms and their physical (abiotic) environment, at four levels: organisms, populations, communities and biomes. Questions can be "how-type" (the mechanism: how does the bulbul sing?) or "why-type" (the significance: why does it sing?).

A population is a group of individuals of a species in a well-defined geographical area that share or compete for similar resources and potentially interbreed (asexually produced groups also count). Examples: cormorants in a wetland, rats in an abandoned dwelling, teakwood trees in a forest tract, bacteria in a culture plate, lotus plants in a pond. Natural selection operates at the population level, so population ecology links ecology to population genetics and evolution.

Topic 2

Population growth

Population density changes through four processes: natality (B) and immigration (I) add individuals; mortality (D) and emigration (E) remove them. Normally births and deaths matter most; immigration matters more when a new habitat is being colonised.

RememberExponential (geometric): unlimited resources, J-shaped curve, dN/dt = rN, Nₜ = N₀eʳᵗ (e = 2.71828). Logistic (Verhulst-Pearl): limited resources, sigmoid curve with lag, acceleration, deceleration and asymptote at K. Logistic is considered more realistic. r values: Norway rat 0.015, flour beetle 0.12, India 1981: 0.0205.
NEET trapExercise 2: a population doubling in 3 years has r = ln 2 / 3 ≈ 0.693 / 3 ≈ 0.231 per year (from 2N₀ = N₀e³ʳ). Darwin showed that even a slow-breeding elephant could reach enormous numbers without checks.
Topic 3

Life history variation

Populations evolve to maximise reproductive (Darwinian) fitness, a high r value, in their habitat, moving towards the most efficient reproductive strategy under their particular selection pressures. Life history traits evolve in relation to the abiotic and biotic constraints of the habitat; this is an active area of research.

StrategyExamples (NCERT)

Topic 4

Population interactions: predation and competition

No habitat holds just one species: even a plant needs soil microbes to recycle nutrients and animals to pollinate it. Interspecific interactions are scored + (beneficial), − (detrimental) or 0 (neutral) for each species. Predation, parasitism and commensalism share one feature: the species live closely together.

Predation transfers the energy fixed by plants to higher trophic levels (a sparrow eating seeds is as much a predator as a tiger; herbivores are not very different). Predators keep prey populations under control; without natural predators, introduced species become invasive: the prickly pear cactus in Australia (1920s) was controlled only by introducing a cactus-feeding moth. Biological pest control rests on this. Predators are 'prudent': overexploiting prey would lead to their own extinction.

Nearly 25% of all insects are phytophagous, and plants cannot run away, hence their elaborate defences.

Competition is best defined as a process in which the fitness (r) of one species is significantly lower in the presence of another. It can occur between unrelated species (flamingoes and resident fishes compete for zooplankton in shallow South American lakes) and even when resources are not limiting (interference competition). Herbivores and plants seem more affected by competition than carnivores. The Abingdon tortoise of the Galapagos became extinct within a decade of goats being introduced.

NEET trapGause's Competitive Exclusion Principle: two closely related species competing for the same resources cannot coexist indefinitely; the inferior one is eliminated. This may hold if resources are limiting, but not otherwise. Species may instead evolve coexistence mechanisms such as resource partitioning (different feeding times or foraging patterns).
Topic 5

Parasitism, commensalism and mutualism

Parasitism (free lodging and meals) has evolved in groups from plants to higher vertebrates. Many parasites are host-specific, so host and parasite co-evolve. Adaptations: loss of unnecessary sense organs, adhesive organs or suckers, loss of digestive system, high reproductive capacity. Life cycles are often complex: the human liver fluke needs two intermediate hosts (a snail and a fish); the malarial parasite needs a vector (mosquito). Most parasites harm the host, reducing its survival, growth, reproduction and population density.

TypeExamples
Ectoparasites (on the surface)Lice on humans, ticks on dogs, copepods on marine fish; Cuscuta on hedge plants (has lost chlorophyll and leaves)
Endoparasites (inside: liver, kidney, lungs, RBCs)More complex life cycles; simplified morphology and anatomy; high reproductive potential
Brood parasitismCuckoo (koel) lays eggs in the crow's nest; its eggs mimic the host's in size and colour
Commensalism (+ / 0)Orchid epiphyte on a mango branch; barnacles on a whale; cattle egret and grazing cattle; clown fish among stinging sea anemone tentacles
Mutualism (+ / +)Lichens (fungus + alga or cyanobacterium); mycorrhizae (fungus helps absorb nutrients, plant gives carbohydrates); fig and wasp; orchids and bees

RememberFig and wasp: a tight one-to-one relationship. A fig species is pollinated only by its partner wasp. The female wasp pollinates the inflorescence while searching for egg-laying sites, lays eggs in the fruit, and her larvae eat some developing seeds: the fig's "fee". Mutualisms must guard against cheaters (nectar thieves), which drives co-evolution.
NEET trapThe female mosquito is not considered a parasite, though it needs our blood for reproduction: it does not live on or in us. An orchid on a mango branch is commensalism (exercise 4). Biological control rests on predation regulating prey (exercise 5).
