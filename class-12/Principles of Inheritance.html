Topic 1

Mendel and the inheritance of one gene

Genetics deals with inheritance (passing characters from parent to progeny, the basis of heredity) and variation (how far progeny differ from parents). People exploited natural variation by artificial selection long ago: Indian breeds such as Sahiwal cows of Punjab come from wild ancestral cows.

Gregor Mendel made hybridisation experiments on garden peas for seven years (1856–1863). For the first time, statistical analysis and mathematical logic were applied to biology; his large sample sizes made the data credible. He used 14 true-breeding varieties (lines that self-pollinate and breed true for generations) as 7 pairs differing in one character.

Test cross: what is this violet plant?

Fig 2 · interactive

After NCERT Fig 4.5. Violet (W) is dominant over white (w).

RememberLaw of Dominance (first law): (i) characters are controlled by discrete units, factors; (ii) factors occur in pairs; (iii) in a dissimilar pair one member dominates. It explains the F1 and the 3 : 1 F2. Law of Segregation (second law): alleles do not blend; the two factors of a pair segregate at gamete formation so each gamete gets one. A homozygote makes one kind of gamete; a heterozygote, two kinds in equal proportion.
NEET trapA test cross crosses the dominant-phenotype organism with the recessive parent, not with itself. 1 : 1 means the test plant was heterozygous; all dominant means homozygous. Dwarf F2 plants self-pollinated gave only dwarfs in F3 and F4: they were tt.
Topic 2

Beyond simple dominance

Why is an allele dominant? Take a gene for an enzyme that transforms substrate S. A modified allele may make (i) a normal or less efficient enzyme, (ii) a non-functional enzyme, or (iii) no enzyme. In case (i) the alleles are equivalent. In (ii) and (iii) the phenotype depends only on the unmodified allele, which is therefore dominant; the modified allele is usually recessive.

RememberCo-dominance: the F1 resembles both parents (IᴬIᴮ makes both A and B sugars on RBCs). Incomplete dominance: F1 is in between (Rr pink); the genotype ratio stays 1 : 2 : 1 and the phenotype ratio becomes 1 : 2 : 1 too. ABO is also multiple alleles: more than two alleles for one character, visible only in population studies because a person carries just two.
NEET trapPea starch gene (B, b): BB round seeds, large starch grains; bb wrinkled, small grains; Bb round but intermediate grains. Seed shape shows dominance; grain size shows incomplete dominance. So dominance is not an autonomous feature of a gene: it depends on which phenotype you examine.
Topic 3

Inheritance of two genes

Crossing yellow-round (RRYY) with green-wrinkled (rryy) gave all yellow-round F1 (RrYy). Selfing the F1 gave 9 round yellow : 3 wrinkled yellow : 3 round green : 1 wrinkled green, which is (3 round : 1 wrinkled) × (3 yellow : 1 green). Law of Independent Assortment: "when two pairs of traits are combined in a hybrid, segregation of one pair of characters is independent of the other pair". The F1 makes four gametes, RY, Ry, rY, ry, each ¼.

Topic 4

Chromosomal theory, linkage and recombination

Mendel published in 1865, but his work stayed unrecognised until 1900: communication was poor; discrete non-blending factors seemed to clash with apparently continuous variation; mathematics in biology was unacceptable to many; and he had no physical proof of factors. In 1900 de Vries, Correns and von Tschermak independently rediscovered his results. By 1902 chromosome movement in meiosis was worked out, and Walter Sutton and Theodore Boveri saw that chromosomes behave like genes. Sutton united the two as the chromosomal theory of inheritance, verified experimentally by Thomas Hunt Morgan.

Alfred Sturtevant made the first genetic map.
Topic 5

Polygenic inheritance and pleiotropy

Polygenic traits spread across a gradient (human height, skin colour). They are generally controlled by three or more genes, are influenced by the environment, and the effect of each allele is additive. Pleiotropy is the reverse: one gene, several phenotypic effects, usually through metabolic pathways.

Topic 6

Sex determination

Henking (1891) traced a nuclear structure through spermatogenesis in some insects: 50% of sperm received it. He called it the X body; it was later shown to be a chromosome, the X chromosome. Chromosomes involved in sex determination are sex chromosomes; the rest are autosomes.

Topic 7

Mutation and Mendelian disorders

Mutation alters DNA sequence and so genotype and phenotype; with recombination it is a source of variation. Loss (deletion) or gain (insertion, duplication) of a DNA segment alters chromosomes; such aberrations are common in cancer cells. A change in a single base pair is a point mutation (sickle-cell anaemia). Deletions and insertions of base pairs cause frame-shift mutations. Agents that induce mutation are mutagens, e.g. UV radiation.

Controlled crosses are impossible in humans, so traits are traced through family trees: pedigree analysis. Mendelian disorders come from a mutation in a single gene and may be dominant or recessive, autosomal or sex-linked: haemophilia, cystic fibrosis, sickle-cell anaemia, colour blindness, phenylketonuria, thalassemia. Pedigree example of an autosomal dominant trait: myotonic dystrophy.

Topic 8

Chromosomal disorders

Caused by absence, excess or abnormal arrangement of chromosomes. Failure of chromatids to segregate gives gain or loss of chromosomes: aneuploidy (trisomy, monosomy). Failure of cytokinesis after telophase adds a whole set: polyploidy, often seen in plants.
